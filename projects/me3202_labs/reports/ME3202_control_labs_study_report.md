## Lab 3: signal spectrum

**Aim.** Identify the frequencies and relative amplitudes of a sampled signal. The program generates `sin(2*pi*5*t) + 0.5*sin(2*pi*12*t)` at 64 samples/s for 128 samples. It evaluates the discrete Fourier transform directly and reports one-sided magnitudes. This is a DFT implementation, even though the lab topic is commonly described as FFT analysis.

**Run.** `python projects/me3202_labs/fft_signal/fft_demo.py`

**Computed result.** The bin spacing is 0.50 Hz. Peaks occur at 5.0 Hz (amplitude 1.00) and 12.0 Hz (amplitude 0.50). The generated `results/spectrum.csv` can be plotted in Excel or MATLAB.

**Interpretation.** With `N=128` and `fs=64 Hz`, resolution is `fs/N=0.5 Hz`; Nyquist frequency is 32 Hz. Lowering the sampling rate below twice the highest frequency risks aliasing. A non-bin-centred tone would spread energy across bins unless windowed.

## Lab 7: two-link arm PID

**Aim.** Track joint angle references while accounting for manipulator inertia, velocity coupling and gravity. The model solves `M(q) qdd + C(q,qd) qd + G(q) = tau`. A PID term with gravity compensation generates joint torque, limited to +/-15 N m. Link and mass values are illustrative and documented in the [code](../two_link_pid/simulate.py).

**Run.** `python projects/me3202_labs/two_link_pid/simulate.py`

**Computed result.** For a 3.0 s step to `(0.4000, -0.2500)` rad, the final positions are `(0.4034, -0.2514)` rad. Absolute errors are `(0.0034, 0.0014)` rad. `results/joint_response.csv` contains time, both joint positions, targets and torques. The result is from the Python model, not from the original MATLAB/Simulink lab or robot hardware.

**Interpretation.** Proportional gain reduces position error; derivative action damps motion; integral action compensates residual bias but can wind up. Torque limiting and gravity compensation should be explained when comparing tuning. The original lab also asks for ramp and sinusoidal references, which are future extensions to this step-response study.

## Lab 9: root locus

**Aim.** Examine how proportional gain changes closed-loop poles. The manual gives `G(s)=1/(s^2+3s+2)`; for unity negative feedback, the characteristic equation is `s^2+3s+(2+K)=0`.

**Run.** `python projects/me3202_labs/root_locus/root_locus_demo.py`

**Computed result.** At `K=0`, poles are `-1` and `-2`. They meet at `-1.5` for `K=0.25`; at `K=1`, they are `-1.5 +/- 0.8660j`. For all positive gains in this model the pole real parts remain `-1.5` after the breakaway, so the continuous-time linear model remains stable. The output file `results/root_locus.csv` can be plotted in the complex plane.

## Labs 1, 2, 4 and 5: interview notes

- **Lab 1:** For successive free-vibration peaks of equal sign, logarithmic decrement is `delta = ln(x_n/x_(n+1))`. For an underdamped SDOF oscillator, `zeta = delta / sqrt(4*pi^2 + delta^2)`. Estimate from several peak pairs to reduce noise. No measured peak values are supplied here.
- **Lab 2:** A frequency sweep reveals resonance near natural frequencies. Record excitation frequency, displacement/acceleration response, and sensor placement before comparing modes. No measured sweep is supplied here.
- **Lab 4:** Velocity feedback changes effective damping; displacement feedback changes effective stiffness. Feedback sign and magnitude determine whether the response is better damped or destabilized. No measured spectrum is supplied here.
- **Lab 5:** For `m*xdd + c*xd + k*x = F`, natural frequency is `sqrt(k/m)` and damping ratio is `c/(2*sqrt(k*m))`. The forced response depends on frequency ratio and damping; resonance peak falls as damping rises. No personal MATLAB output is supplied here.
